How the ceiling “collapsed” — and what actually happened

The short version we used in conversation — “the classical ceiling collapsed to 0.000” — was misleading shorthand, and it is worth being precise because the distinction is the whole finding.

Our actual rule still scores 0.0899 — three times random. What collapses to zero is the procedure that picks a rule. Those are different objects and we conflated them.

The chain of events, in order

DefectOur fpocket parser read residues as int(line[22:26]), discarding the chain. Nine of twenty benchmark rows have cross-chain residue-number collisions, so pocket residues silently mapped onto the wrong chain. Found by your agents, confirmed by us.
EffectOne target’s oracle was a perfect 1.000 purely because a chain-B cavity landed on chain-A ground truth. Raw oracle fell 0.556 → 0.454.
Knock-onThe sensible rule’s in-sample score fell 0.1649 → 0.0899. A different rule, hop≥3 + lex_far_first, scored 0.100 — and won the sweep by 0.0101.
Why it’s zeroThat rule scores above zero on exactly two of twenty rows — and those two rows are the same apo structure. Its entire mean is one structure counted twice. On every held-out target it scores 0.000.
Root causeThe selection criterion is a raw mean over pseudo-replicated rows, with no cluster correction. A rule that nails one structure beats a rule that works on four. Ours works on four.

We tested the fix: selecting by cluster mean instead of row mean moves the held-out score from 0.0000 to 0.0093 and the p-value from 0.00024 to 0.101. It removes the pathology. It does not produce a working selector.

The honest statement

At pocket level, nothing beats chance — not CTQW (p = 1.000), not a fixed classical rule (p = 0.220). Automated rule selection on 13 clusters actively backfires. Meanwhile the oracle sits at 0.398: good candidates exist in every protein, and no procedure we have — classical or quantum — finds them.

What replaced it, and why it is stronger

Your section-08 observation — that the benchmark is mostly contact-adjacent pockets, so a transport operator cannot really be tested on it — turned out to be the most important thing either of us found. We took it to the field’s own benchmarks.

For every structure we computed the minimum heavy-atom distance between the annotated allosteric site and the annotated catalytic site, using each benchmark’s own annotations. No fpocket, no detection, no CTQW — only the distance.

1.5 Å is shorter than a carbon–carbon bond. These are sites covalently bonded to the catalytic site. Twelve ASBench structures share residues between the two outright.

158 proteins, three cohorts, and our benchmark sits in the middle. This is not a quirk of the Cleveland Clinic target list — it is a property of how allosteric sites are annotated in this field. Only half of ASBench is genuinely distal.

Bond-to-bond’s own failure analysis says the same thing from the other side: on PDB 1Z8D it records that “direct interactions, instead of functional coupling, occur if sites are close to the orthosteric sites, which is out of the scope of bond-to-bond propensity analysis.” The method excludes these cases by definition. The benchmarks are full of them.

The distinction

Their six measures are set-level enrichment tests, not top-k retrieval tests. Each asks whether the annotated site’s average score beats surrogate sites of the same size. “84% allosteric-site recovery” therefore does not mean “finds the pocket.”

On retrieval, the published state of the art is 0.0204 against a 0.0176 chance baseline. Nobody has solved this — including them, and including us.

This is not a criticism of their work. It does what it claims and it beats chance decisively. What we are correcting is the framing of the number, and the correction is what makes the benchmark problem visible.

The physics encoded in the Hamiltonian is doing real work. What fails is the propagation step layered on top of it — not the model of the protein underneath.

Our variance attribution puts the quantum contribution at roughly 5–10% on average (range 1–15% across targets), against 20–60% for geometry and 25–80% unexplained. Small, variable, but not zero — and it is that measured share, not a preference, that set our position.

Two consequences we want stated explicitly in the submission:

  • Credit where it is due. The two-stage design and the Hamiltonian work are yours, and the 0.751 result is the strongest constructive number either of us has. It should be reported as a positive finding, not as context for a negative.
  • The negative is narrow and should stay narrow. It concerns the time-averaged occupation observable on one operator — as you noted first. It is not a claim about quantum walks as a class, and we will not write it as one.

At +18.4% on 108 structures, the quantum contribution is real, and it is larger than the 5–10% our 13-structure attribution estimated. It holds at eight times the cohort size, and it is more than an order of magnitude above bond-to-bond’s +1.3% on this metric.

Corrected

The single strongest predictor is proximity — the exact inverse of the field’s starting intuition.

Ranking residues by distance from the active site scores 0.3853 — far worse than guessing. Flip the sign and the same quantity is the best arm in the table at 0.6147, ahead of the quantum walk and twenty times the published method.

This is Finding F restated from the ranking side, and it is the sharpest form of it: allosteric sites in these benchmarks are not merely often proximal — proximity is the most informative single thing you can know about where they are.

Headroom

77.1% of the available signal is explained by nothing we have.

The best single arm captures 22.9%. Neither the quantum walk, nor packing, nor flexibility, nor distance, nor the published state of the art accounts for the rest. That is not a negative result — it is a measured target, and it is the honest case for Phase 2.

Two corrections made while producing this table. First, our initial version scored distance in one direction only and therefore reported CTQW as the strongest arm; it is second. Second, our first run scored the published method at exactly AUC 0.5000, which contradicted our own earlier positive control (p = 5×10⁻⁹). The cause was ours — their per-residue files are keyed to their stripped structures and we had mapped them onto deposited numbering. Recomputed on their own keys the figure is 0.5067, and that is the number reported.

The discriminator idea — where it actually stands

This is the thread worth keeping. It has moved twice since we last spoke, in opposite directions.

Gate passed

The six measures are genuinely independent evidence.

We had killed the idea of combining rankers on our own cohort, for a specific reason: all 61 of our rules were functions of the same two inputs — distance and druggability. They were not 61 opinions but one opinion at 61 settings, so ensembling them provably could not work.

The six measures are not that. Mean pairwise |φ| = 0.419, under our pre-registered 0.6 redundancy bar, with no residue/bond block collapse. This is the first genuinely independent evidence family either of us has had.

Open

But we cannot yet predict which measure fires.

The gap between 99/118 by one measure and 21/118 by all six is 78 structures where some fire and others do not — exactly the signature of “different measures suit different proteins.”

Protein size, chain count and site separation carry no signal about which. Held out by protein, per-measure AUC 0.337–0.483. A positive control — predicting one measure from the other five — reaches AUC 0.74–0.88, so the harness detects real signal when it is there.

Fold class was the obvious remaining predictor. We tested it during the hour before this call, and it fails too. CATH/SCOP class pulled live for 111 of 113 ASBench structures: no measure’s firing rate differs by class after correction (best p = 0.025 against a Bonferroni bar of 0.0083), and the number of measures firing does not differ either (2.93 vs 3.65, p = 0.14). One descriptive curiosity, not a finding: mainly-alpha proteins almost always get something to fire (1/23 score zero, against 17/88 for alpha-beta).

So every cheap protein-level predictor is now exhausted — size, chain count, site separation, fold class. The discriminator is not dead, but it will not come from a lookup. It needs either a genuinely new descriptor or the per-residue propensity fields themselves, which is Phase-2 work.

Why this matters for Phase 2: on our own cohort a per-protein meta-selector would lift expected hits from 0.100 to 0.331 — 83% of everything achievable. The rankers are not the bottleneck. Choosing between them is. And with 432 structures over 146 proteins, that question is finally answerable.

Two other independent-signal candidates were tested and failed this week: sibling-conformer pocket persistence (p = 0.969 / 0.856, and exactly zero as a tie-break), and ENM mode shift, which did not reproduce once ported into our apparatus (Fisher p = 0.306 against a claimed 0.005–0.013). Mode shift is independent of druggability once pocket size is controlled — but that is now its only merit.

One more thing, from the organisers

Cleveland Clinic answered our constraint questions today. Both permissively — and the first one matters more than a permission:

“the challenge explicitly assumes the elastic network hypothesis, which posits that the topology of the contact network is the primary driver of signal propagation. Therefore, utilizing closed-form elastic-network (ANM/GNM) mode perfectly fits with the scope of the challenge.”

That makes our central negative a finding about the challenge’s own stated premise, not a side observation. We measured topology-driven propagation against the field’s benchmarks and it does not retrieve the annotated sites.

They also confirmed PocketMiner is permitted — static PDB at inference, no trajectories. Our exclusion of CryptoSite stands and is better justified: it runs MD sampling at inference, which is exactly what the constraint forbids.

Seven places your reproduction will legitimately diverge

  • Chain-aware pocket parsing. fpocket residues must be keyed on (chain, resnum), not int(line[22:26]). Nine of our twenty rows carry cross-chain residue-number collisions; discarding the chain silently maps a cavity onto the wrong subunit. This defect produced the retraction at the top of this brief.
  • Score every direction. For a strict inversion AUC(−s) = 1 − AUC(s). We scored distance farthest-first only and consequently mis-ranked the arms in an earlier draft of this page. If an arm looks strongly below chance, its inverse is your best arm.
  • Terminal exclusion. Flexibility and packing arms rank chain termini first unless the terminal 5% is excluded. Without it, every arm is handicapped against a metric that does not exclude them.
  • Their per-residue files are keyed to their stripped structures. Mapping bond-to-bond propensity onto deposited numbering yields exactly AUC 0.5000 — a silent, plausible-looking null. Score on their keys.
  • Cluster by protein, not structure. Our 20 rows are 13 structures; CASBench’s 314 are 33 proteins. Four selection procedures in our register were destroyed by ignoring this.
  • Enrichment is not retrieval. The six published measures test whether a site’s average score beats surrogate sites. That is not top-k retrieval, and the two disagree sharply.
  • Getting the data. PMC serves the supplementary tables behind a JS proof-of-work challenge; Europe PMC’s supplementaryFiles endpoint does not. The 2.55 GB figshare archive need not be downloaded — a range request for its last 2 MB returns the zip central directory, and individual files can be pulled by offset.
